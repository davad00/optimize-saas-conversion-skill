# 66 persuasion patterns

The original 22 mechanisms plus 44 additional applied patterns. Select by the buyer's observed obstacle; these are application hypotheses, not guaranteed effects.

Read the [selection playbook](references/persuasion-patterns.md) and [primary-source register](references/persuasion-evidence.md).

## PAT-001 — Processing fluency

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** Visitors misread the product, billing unit, or next action even though the facts are present.

**Apply:** Rewrite the decision in familiar task vocabulary, use one stable name per concept, and put the direct answer before supporting explanation. Keep required qualifications beside the claim; compare comprehension before and after.

**Example:** Illustrative copy: “Review a contract and export the marked-up document” replaces “Accelerate intelligence workflows.”

**Mechanism:** Reducing interpretation effort may let buyers evaluate fit without repeatedly translating jargon.

**Check:** Check comprehension with representative tasks. Do not remove meaningful limits or assume simpler copy changes the underlying offer.

**Measure:** Correct explanation of product and commitment; qualified paid conversion and expectation-related support.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis. Readability and clarity are useful design goals; this record does not assert a universal fluency-driven sales effect.

**Sources:** Application hypothesis; no direct source assigned.

**Related tactics:** CPY-001, CPY-015, CPY-017

## PAT-002 — Specificity

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** Claims sound interchangeable because they omit the real task, output, audience, or operating condition.

**Apply:** Replace generic praise with a verifiable action and result. Name the intended buyer, input, useful output, and relevant dependency. Put a real artifact beside the claim when available.

**Example:** Illustrative structure: “Turn [supported input] into [inspectable output] for [buyer task], after [required setup].”

**Mechanism:** A concrete promise may help buyers recognize their own task and test whether the product can perform it.

**Check:** Confirm the exact capability, plan, and prerequisite. Precision must describe product truth rather than invented performance.

**Measure:** Offer comprehension, appropriate signup or purchase, and mismatch-related refunds.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis. Specific wording is not itself proof of accuracy or an experimentally established uplift.

**Sources:** Application hypothesis; no direct source assigned.

**Related tactics:** POS-005, POS-015, CPY-002

## PAT-003 — Salience and contrast

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** People can describe the offer but miss the purchase action or selected state in a visually crowded page.

**Apply:** Give the primary action a distinct visual role using existing brand tokens. Reduce competing accents, group terms with their action, and keep essential selection and status cues available beyond color.

**Example:** Illustrative treatment: one filled primary button, quieter supporting links, and a visible selected-plan label.

**Mechanism:** Attention directed toward the relevant decision may reduce search effort and accidental actions.

**Check:** Test the rendered surface, keyboard focus, narrow layout, and contrast. A contrast calculation cannot establish overall usability or sales impact.

**Measure:** Task completion, misselection, and paid conversion; accessibility and error guardrails.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis with accessibility requirements in PE-023; no universal best purchasing color is implied.

**Sources:** PE-023

**Related tactics:** VIS-001, VIS-002, VIS-008, CTA-003

## PAT-004 — Social proof

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** Buyers doubt whether other relevant customers obtain useful results from the product.

**Apply:** Use approved, attributable evidence from a comparable buying situation. Place each case or review near the specific doubt it answers, and preserve context, dates, and access to the fuller account.

**Example:** Illustrative placement: a verified small-team implementation story beside the setup section rather than an unexplained logo wall.

**Mechanism:** Other customers’ observable experience may inform an uncertain buyer’s evaluation.

**Check:** Confirm permission, relationship, current relevance, and scope. Do not infer popularity or causality from a selected testimonial.

**Measure:** Qualified purchase, comprehension of proof, activation, and early churn.

**Evidence:** Research-linked mechanism: PE-010 studies social influence in online ratings; this testimonial application remains untested for the site.

**Sources:** PE-010

**Related tactics:** PRF-001, PRF-002, PRF-004

## PAT-005 — Authority

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** Evaluation depends on a specialist claim that the buyer cannot easily verify unaided.

**Apply:** Present the relevant expert explanation, technical document, or scoped certification and let the buyer inspect it. State who issued it, what product or process it covers, and what it does not establish.

**Example:** Illustrative copy: “[Named report] covers [specific service and period]; view scope and exclusions.”

**Mechanism:** Relevant expertise can supply a reasoned basis for confidence in a difficult technical judgment.

**Check:** Verify current scope, credential, independence, and applicability. A logo or unrelated credential cannot substantiate every product claim.

**Measure:** Completion of the blocked evaluation step, qualified purchases, and avoidable procurement questions.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis. Treat authority as inspectable evidence, not a guarantee that endorsement causes purchase.

**Sources:** Application hypothesis; no direct source assigned.

**Related tactics:** PRF-012, PRF-013, SLS-012

## PAT-006 — Anchoring

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** Buyers lack a meaningful reference for judging the subscription price or delivered value.

**Apply:** Place a verified, comparable reference beside the offer: the current workflow cost, a useful higher-service package, or a valid prior price. Explain differences and keep all numerical assumptions inspectable.

**Example:** Illustrative comparison: the buyer’s entered cost for replaceable tools next to the subscription and any tools still required.

**Mechanism:** A relevant reference point may shape valuation and make the trade-off easier to assess.

**Check:** Avoid inflated reference prices, incomparable workloads, or omitted costs. Confirm the anchor is applicable to this buyer.

**Measure:** Paid conversion and contribution per eligible buyer, plan suitability, and refunds.

**Evidence:** Research-linked mechanism: PE-001 concerns judgment anchoring; it does not predict a SaaS price or conversion uplift.

**Sources:** PE-001

**Related tactics:** PRI-007, PRI-017, PRI-019

## PAT-007 — Contrast and compromise

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** Multiple viable packages serve different needs, but buyers cannot see their meaningful trade-offs.

**Apply:** Arrange real options around comparable requirements and explain why each exists. Show the distinctive reason for a higher tier and a truthful use case for the entry tier; recommend only from the buyer’s stated needs.

**Example:** Illustrative labels: “For individual review” and “For teams needing shared approvals,” when those capabilities are verified.

**Mechanism:** Choice context can change the perceived attraction of a middle or comparatively suitable option.

**Check:** Every offered package must be useful on its own. Do not equate a change in highlighted-plan share with more demand.

**Measure:** Overall paid conversion, net value per eligible unit, plan fit, and subsequent downgrades.

**Evidence:** Research-linked mechanisms: PE-003 examines asymmetric dominance; PE-025 addresses tradeoff contrast and extremeness aversion. Neither establishes the right SaaS packages or incremental demand.

**Sources:** PE-003, PE-025

**Related tactics:** PKG-001, PKG-002, DEC-017

## PAT-008 — Risk reversal

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** Appropriate buyers hesitate because they cannot resolve an important uncertainty before commitment.

**Apply:** Use an existing preview, bounded trial, assisted pilot, or operable guarantee to let the buyer evaluate that uncertainty. If new commercial terms are needed, propose them with eligibility, limits, fulfillment ownership, and cost.

**Example:** Illustrative offer structure: “Evaluate [defined workload] against [success criteria] before [actual commitment].”

**Mechanism:** Reducing a real downside of evaluation may make a suitable purchase easier to justify.

**Check:** Confirm the promise can be honored and explain payment, renewal, refund, and exit conditions accurately.

**Measure:** Net retained value, trial-to-paid or pilot-to-paid outcomes, refunds, abuse, and service cost.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis. Removing risk has operational costs and does not guarantee incremental profitable customers.

**Sources:** Application hypothesis; no direct source assigned.

**Related tactics:** OFR-001, OFR-005, SLS-010

## PAT-009 — Reciprocity

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** The product’s expertise is hard to assess before purchase, and a small useful artifact can demonstrate it.

**Apply:** Provide a genuinely usable sample, template, diagnostic, or lesson that solves a bounded part of the buyer’s problem. Explain any data exchange before collection and connect the artifact to a relevant next task.

**Example:** Illustrative resource: an editable audit worksheet with a clear explanation of which recurring work the paid product automates.

**Mechanism:** A helpful exchange may encourage attention and continued evaluation while demonstrating actual competence.

**Check:** Do not make the resource useless without a purchase or silently enroll recipients in outreach. Publishing and messaging follow the task’s authorized scope.

**Measure:** Use of the artifact, qualified product activation, paid outcomes, and unwanted-contact complaints.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis. A free resource can attract poor-fit demand; no universal reciprocal purchase effect is assumed.

**Sources:** Application hypothesis; no direct source assigned.

**Related tactics:** OFR-018, INT-005, DEM-012

## PAT-010 — Commitment and consistency

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** A visitor has a relevant goal but the next steps are disconnected from that goal.

**Apply:** Let the user choose a useful task or working need and carry that preference into the next screen. Make the preference editable and skip unnecessary commitments that do not improve setup or decision quality.

**Example:** Illustrative sequence: choose “review my first document,” then open that task with its required input ready.

**Mechanism:** Continuity with a voluntary goal may help the user follow through on a relevant intention.

**Check:** A small choice is not consent to purchase, contact, or a larger commitment. Preserve an easy change of mind.

**Measure:** Completion of the chosen task, time to first value, and paid conversion without added setup abandonment.

**Evidence:** Research-linked mechanism: PE-009 studies sequential requests; this product flow remains an application hypothesis.

**Sources:** PE-009

**Related tactics:** ACT-001, CTA-006, DEC-019

## PAT-011 — Ownership and investment

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** Buyers understand claims but have not experienced creating something useful with the product.

**Apply:** Let them personalize a real preview or create a meaningful first artifact, then explain what is saved, exportable, and included. Preserve completed work across ordinary interruptions and authorized upgrade paths.

**Example:** Illustrative task: edit a sample report, inspect the changed output, and keep the permitted result.

**Mechanism:** Participation and successful creation may increase perceived relevance and value.

**Check:** Do not add purposeless labor or make work inaccessible to manufacture pressure. State genuine free and paid boundaries before work begins.

**Measure:** Successful artifact creation, repeat use, paid conversion, and abandonment after effort.

**Evidence:** Research-linked mechanism: PE-008 studies valuation of self-created objects and completion conditions; SaaS transfer is a hypothesis.

**Sources:** PE-008

**Related tactics:** DEM-013, ACT-003, UPG-002

## PAT-012 — Progress and goal proximity

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** A meaningful setup or evaluation task contains several steps and users cannot tell what remains.

**Apply:** Show completed useful steps and the real remaining work. Credit work already performed by the user or system accurately, and make the next necessary action clear without padding the checklist.

**Example:** Illustrative checklist: source connected, sample verified, first report pending—not cosmetic profile chores.

**Mechanism:** Visible progress toward a real goal may support persistence and reduce uncertainty about remaining effort.

**Check:** Completion percentages must correspond to actual criteria. Do not represent an account created as successful product value.

**Measure:** Milestone completion, time to first useful result, paid conversion, and unnecessary-step abandonment.

**Evidence:** Research-linked mechanism: PE-006 examines progress toward rewards; this checklist is a site-specific application.

**Sources:** PE-006

**Related tactics:** ACT-009, DEC-006, SIG-015

## PAT-013 — Defaults

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** A reversible starting choice can be reasonably inferred for a new undecided buyer.

**Apply:** Choose a visible, reasoned starting option and retain explicit user preferences, valid campaign context, account entitlements, and experiment assignment. For billing, follow the mandatory pricing gate and present the actual commitment clearly.

**Example:** Illustrative use: a suitable discounted annual option selected initially, while a deliberate monthly selection remains selected on return.

**Mechanism:** A starting state can reduce decision effort and influence the option considered first.

**Check:** No default authorizes payment, a subscription change, or hidden add-ons. Test fresh state, selection changes, and return paths.

**Measure:** Overall paid conversion and retained economics, mistaken commitments, and refunds—not default-option share alone.

**Evidence:** Research-linked mechanism: PE-004 examines organ-donation defaults; annual-first effectiveness must be assessed in the actual buying context.

**Sources:** PE-004

**Related tactics:** DEC-007, CTA-006, PAY-001

## PAT-014 — Loss framing

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** The current workflow has an evidenced recurring cost that is relevant to the buyer’s decision.

**Apply:** Explain the concrete cost of continuing the current process alongside the achievable benefit of changing it. Use the buyer’s own assumptions when estimating time or money and show what the product cannot remove.

**Example:** Illustrative structure: “[Observed rework] requires [measured effort]; this workflow addresses [verified portion].”

**Mechanism:** Reference-dependent evaluation may make an existing cost more noticeable than an abstract future benefit.

**Check:** Do not invent losses, claim every buyer is falling behind, or treat saved time as automatically realized cash.

**Measure:** Qualified paid conversion, expectation match, net value, and fear- or pressure-related complaints.

**Evidence:** Research-linked mechanism: PE-002 models decisions under risk; it supplies neither a universal loss multiplier nor a SaaS uplift.

**Sources:** PE-002

**Related tactics:** CPY-005, CPY-006, PRI-006

## PAT-015 — Real scarcity

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** An actual capacity constraint affects access to a service, cohort, or assisted setup.

**Apply:** Explain the constrained resource, current availability, allocation method, and next opportunity. Derive availability from the real operating source and update it when places are taken or released.

**Example:** Illustrative wording: “[Available] assisted setup appointments this week; view the current booking calendar.”

**Mechanism:** A real limit may make timing and opportunity cost relevant to an interested buyer.

**Check:** Do not invent stock for unlimited software or reuse a fixed low count. A place is reserved only under the actual reservation rules.

**Measure:** Net purchases or completed bookings, cancellations, service delivery, and expectation-related support.

**Evidence:** Research-linked mechanism: PE-012 reviews scarcity and value; the operational application and its sales effect require local evidence.

**Sources:** PE-012

**Related tactics:** OFR-008, SLS-018

## PAT-016 — Real urgency

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** A genuine deadline changes the available offer or the buyer’s ability to meet an important date.

**Apply:** State the exact deadline, timezone, reason, and post-deadline behavior. Keep the deadline consistent across the page, checkout, devices, and messages that are authorized to be sent.

**Example:** Illustrative wording: “Enroll by [actual date] for [real cohort start]; later applications join [next available intake].”

**Mechanism:** A consequential time boundary can help an already interested buyer decide when to act.

**Check:** Do not reset the timer or imply that routine software availability expires. Distinguish a buyer’s chosen target from a seller-imposed deadline.

**Measure:** Net paid outcomes, cancellations, support burden, and fulfillment against the promised date.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis. The existence of a deadline does not establish an urgency-driven revenue gain.

**Sources:** Application hypothesis; no direct source assigned.

**Related tactics:** OFR-009, DEC-016, SLS-018

## PAT-017 — Mental accounting and price framing

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** The billable unit or payment interval obscures how the offer fits the buyer’s budget.

**Apply:** Show the comparison unit buyers need while preserving the real amount charged, commitment duration, and required extras. Explain the unit with exact arithmetic and label rounded equivalents.

**Example:** Illustrative structure: “[Monthly equivalent]/month, [annual amount] billed yearly,” followed by the real amount due at checkout.

**Mechanism:** Budget framing may make affordability and trade-offs easier to understand.

**Check:** Compute equivalents from actual prices. Never hide total commitment behind a small daily number or equate annual cash with monthly earned revenue.

**Measure:** Price comprehension, paid conversion, net retained value, and billing-related refunds.

**Evidence:** Research-linked mechanism: PE-005 models mental accounting; it does not prescribe one best price frame for every segment.

**Sources:** PE-005

**Related tactics:** PRI-008, PRI-009, CPY-008

## PAT-018 — Implementation intentions

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** A willing user knows what they want but does not translate that intention into a first concrete use.

**Apply:** Offer an optional plan connecting a real task, required input, and suitable time or cue. Show the plan on return and make it easy to edit; reminders require the appropriate authorized preference-based flow.

**Example:** Illustrative prompt: “When [work event] happens, use [input] to complete [first useful task].”

**Mechanism:** Linking a cue with a specific action may help a person enact an existing goal.

**Check:** Do not force scheduling before demonstrating value, invent calendar commitments, or send unrequested reminders.

**Measure:** First-task completion and time to value, subsequent paid conversion, and setup abandonment.

**Evidence:** Research-linked mechanism: PE-007 discusses cue-linked action planning; the particular SaaS onboarding implementation remains a hypothesis.

**Sources:** PE-007

**Related tactics:** ACT-020, RET-018

## PAT-019 — Autonomy

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** The journey pushes users into a configuration or commitment they cannot easily inspect or change.

**Apply:** Make consequential choices explicit, keep relevant preferences editable, and provide understandable ways to skip optional setup or decline an offer. Let the user continue a useful eligible route after declining.

**Example:** Illustrative behavior: dismiss an upgrade suggestion and return to the saved current-plan task without losing work.

**Mechanism:** Perceived control may reduce resistance and help buyers make a sustainable choice.

**Check:** Check that declining, cancellation, and changing preferences actually work. Do not use humiliating labels or preselected extra charges.

**Measure:** Appropriate conversion, repeated satisfied use, accidental purchases, and cancellation-related support.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis. User control is a product requirement and potential trust mechanism, not a guaranteed purchase lever.

**Sources:** Application hypothesis; no direct source assigned.

**Related tactics:** DEC-020, UPG-012, RET-009

## PAT-020 — Anticipation and curiosity

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** The product can reveal a meaningful result, but the page describes it without making it tangible.

**Apply:** Preview a specific useful artifact and explain what a short demo will answer. Reveal enough to make the next action informed, then connect the actual result to a relevant continuation.

**Example:** Illustrative invitation: “See how the reviewed clause links back to its source” with an inspectable sample.

**Mechanism:** An answerable information gap may motivate a buyer to explore a useful capability.

**Check:** Do not hide price, function, or commitment behind clickbait. The revealed result must match the invitation and current product.

**Measure:** Demo completion, relevant activation, downstream purchases, and disappointment after reveal.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis. Curiosity-driven clicks alone are not evidence of improved conversion quality.

**Sources:** Application hypothesis; no direct source assigned.

**Related tactics:** DEM-001, DEM-011, CTA-005

## PAT-021 — Familiarity

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** Unusual labels or controls make an otherwise ordinary buying task feel difficult or unpredictable.

**Apply:** Reuse recognizable interaction conventions and the customer’s task language while preserving the brand. Keep names and selected-state behavior consistent across landing, signup, billing, and account surfaces.

**Example:** Illustrative behavior: clicking the label “Yearly” always selects Yearly, and clicking it again leaves that choice unchanged.

**Mechanism:** Recognition and predictable behavior may reduce the amount buyers must learn before acting.

**Check:** Do not imitate another company’s identity or let familiarity conceal a different charge or commitment. Test keyboard and pointer behavior.

**Measure:** Task completion, misclicks, error recovery, and paid outcomes.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis. Familiar controls support usability; familiarity alone does not establish a sales effect.

**Sources:** Application hypothesis; no direct source assigned.

**Related tactics:** DEC-007, CPY-017, ACT-012

## PAT-022 — Expectation management

**Family:** foundations

**Use when:** Purchase success depends on setup, limits, human work, or conditions missing from the promise.

**Apply:** Explain the first payoff, its prerequisites, relevant service limits, and the work the buyer must still perform. Keep this information near the decision and mirror it in onboarding and sales handoff.

**Example:** Illustrative wording: “[Useful output] after [required input and setup]; [important limitation] still applies.”

**Mechanism:** A realistic mental model may reduce uncertainty before purchase and disappointment afterward.

**Check:** Check promises against actual entitlements, cumulative caps, current capacity, and service ownership. A disclaimer cannot repair a contradictory headline.

**Measure:** Qualified purchase, activation, refunds, fulfillment failures, and expectation-related support.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis. Clear expectations may change who buys; evaluate retained value and suitability alongside initial conversion.

**Sources:** Application hypothesis; no direct source assigned.

**Related tactics:** POS-015, OBJ-009, SLS-016

## PAT-023 — Causal walkthrough

**Family:** comprehension

**Use when:** Visitors understand the claimed result but cannot explain how the product could produce it.

**Apply:** Show the short causal chain from supported input through the actual operation to an inspectable result. Identify automation, human decisions, and external dependencies separately; link each stage to a real demonstration or document.

**Example:** Illustrative sequence: “Connect a source → select matching rules → review suggested matches → approve the export.”

**Mechanism:** A plausible, inspectable explanation may replace an unsupported leap from feature name to promised outcome.

**Check:** Do not depict unshipped steps or imply that a generated suggestion is a verified decision. Keep causal claims within what the implementation demonstrates.

**Measure:** Accurate explanation of how the product works, qualified activation, and purchase expectation match.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis. A convincing causal story is not evidence that the promised effect occurs.

**Sources:** Application hypothesis; no direct source assigned.

**Related tactics:** POS-002, CPY-011, DEM-008

## PAT-024 — Process rehearsal

**Family:** comprehension

**Use when:** Buyers want the outcome but hesitate because they cannot imagine performing the necessary steps.

**Apply:** Provide a short rehearsal of the buyer’s own actions, inputs, and decision points. Let them walk one representative task with a sample, then carry the task context into the real workflow if they continue.

**Example:** Illustrative prompt: “Choose a sample, mark the exception you would check, then preview the exported result.”

**Mechanism:** Mentally or interactively rehearsing the process may make execution feel more manageable and reveal preparation needs.

**Check:** Use the actual workflow and timing. Do not replace realistic action with a fantasy of effortless outcomes.

**Measure:** Task-start and task-completion rates, time to value, paid conversion, and unexpected setup friction.

**Evidence:** Research-linked mechanism: PE-013 studied process simulation for student exam preparation; transfer to product evaluation remains a hypothesis.

**Sources:** PE-013

**Related tactics:** DEM-002, ACT-010, ACT-020

## PAT-025 — Ordinary-day narrative

**Family:** comprehension

**Use when:** Feature lists fail to show how the subscription would fit a recurring working situation.

**Apply:** Tell one short chronological story about an ordinary supported use: the triggering problem, the buyer’s action, the result, and what they do next. Use a real approved case or label a fictional example clearly.

**Example:** Illustrative story: “At the weekly handoff, import the update, resolve flagged gaps, and send the approved summary.”

**Mechanism:** A coherent sequence may help buyers simulate product use and judge relevance to their own routine.

**Check:** Do not present an invented customer as real or imply typical outcomes from a fictional narrative. Include material setup conditions.

**Measure:** Understanding of recurring value, relevant demo completion, purchase, and repeat-use outcomes.

**Evidence:** Research-linked mechanism: PE-014 studies narrative mental simulation; PE-015 shows imagery appeals can depend on ease of imagining. The SaaS story is an application hypothesis.

**Sources:** PE-014, PE-015

**Related tactics:** CPY-007, POS-014, POS-010

## PAT-026 — Bounded analogy mapping

**Family:** comprehension

**Use when:** The product introduces an unfamiliar concept that buyers repeatedly confuse with something else.

**Apply:** Choose a familiar comparison and map only the genuinely shared relationships. Immediately name the important limit of the analogy, then show the actual control or output so the buyer can move beyond it.

**Example:** Illustrative wording: “Think of this as a review queue: items wait for approval. It does not approve them automatically.”

**Mechanism:** A familiar structure may provide a temporary mental model for understanding a new capability.

**Check:** Test whether the analogy causes a false assumption about ownership, automation, payment, or guarantees. Remove it if the literal explanation works better.

**Measure:** Concept comprehension, fewer explanation requests, appropriate activation, and paid conversion.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis. An analogy is a teaching device, not proof of product equivalence or effectiveness.

**Sources:** Application hypothesis; no direct source assigned.

**Related tactics:** CPY-009, CPY-013, POS-007

## PAT-027 — Same-input joint evaluation

**Family:** comprehension

**Use when:** The product advantage is hard to see when the old and new workflows are described separately.

**Apply:** Compare both complete workflows using the same representative input, acceptance criteria, and workload. Show outputs together, including preparation and review work, and let the buyer inspect the decisive difference.

**Example:** Illustrative view: the same support request handled through the current manual process and the product-assisted process.

**Mechanism:** Joint evaluation may make a concrete difference easier to notice without relying on memory or an abstract superiority claim.

**Check:** Do not use an artificially poor baseline or omit steps from one side. Label staged demonstrations and avoid generalizing a single run.

**Measure:** Correct identification of the difference, qualified evaluations, paid outcomes, and expectation match.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis with comparison research context in PE-016; that study does not validate this workflow demonstration.

**Sources:** PE-016

**Related tactics:** DEM-016, DEM-005, POS-006

## PAT-028 — Two-sided explanation

**Family:** comprehension

**Use when:** A sophisticated buyer distrusts an entirely positive claim or discovers important limitations only later.

**Apply:** State one material limitation beside the relevant benefit, explain the practical consequence, and show the supported way to work within it. Make the limitation decision-useful rather than a trivial confession.

**Example:** Illustrative structure: “Works with [supported format]; [unsupported format] needs [real preparation step] before import.”

**Mechanism:** An informative acknowledgment may make evaluation more credible and help suitable buyers distinguish a manageable constraint from a disqualifier.

**Check:** Do not use a small disclosed weakness to distract from a larger hidden one. Verify the proposed workaround and its cost.

**Measure:** Qualified conversion, comprehension of limitations, onboarding failures, and refunds.

**Evidence:** Research-linked mechanism: PE-017 found product-dependent results for one- versus two-sided messages; no universal credibility or sales gain is assumed.

**Sources:** PE-017

**Related tactics:** PRF-007, CPY-003, POS-004

## PAT-029 — Claim-to-evidence traceability

**Family:** evidence

**Use when:** Buyers see strong claims but cannot find the underlying artifact, method, or source needed to evaluate them.

**Apply:** Attach an inspectable evidence path to each decision-critical claim: the relevant sample, measured method, capability documentation, or approved case. Keep the link anchored to the exact claim and preserve return context.

**Example:** Illustrative control: “Inspect the sample behind this result” opens the matching input, output, and conditions.

**Mechanism:** Verifiability may reduce the need to accept a seller’s assertion on trust alone.

**Check:** Check that the evidence actually supports the stated claim and is current, accessible, and permitted to share. More links do not repair irrelevant evidence.

**Measure:** Resolution of the specific doubt, qualified purchase, and evidence-related support questions.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis. This pattern improves inspectability; it does not imply that every visitor reads evidence or converts because of it.

**Sources:** Application hypothesis; no direct source assigned.

**Related tactics:** PRF-005, PRF-017, DEM-018

## PAT-030 — Independent corroboration

**Family:** evidence

**Use when:** All reassurance comes from repeated versions of the seller’s own claim.

**Apply:** Combine genuinely different evidence types for the same important question, such as a reproducible demonstration, a relevant customer account, and current technical documentation. Explain what each establishes and where sources share an origin.

**Example:** Illustrative bundle: a working export sample, a customer’s approved account of using it, and its documented format limits.

**Mechanism:** Converging independent observations may make a conclusion more credible than repeated promotional assertions.

**Check:** Do not count syndicated reviews, paid endorsements, or copied metrics as independent confirmation. Disclose material relationships.

**Measure:** Qualified progress on the blocked buying question, paid conversion, and discrepancy reports.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis. Triangulation strengthens an evidence assessment only when sources are relevant and meaningfully independent.

**Sources:** Application hypothesis; no direct source assigned.

**Related tactics:** PRF-011, PRF-017, PRF-020

## PAT-031 — Typical-outcome calibration

**Family:** evidence

**Use when:** The page highlights a spectacular result that may feel unattainable or create unrealistic expectations.

**Apply:** Lead with a representative outcome distribution or ordinary case, then show exceptional cases with their conditions. Distinguish measured outcomes from illustrative possibilities and disclose sample selection and observation period.

**Example:** Illustrative structure: “For [defined cohort], observed results ranged from [verified bounds]; this case used [specific conditions].”

**Mechanism:** A credible picture of attainable outcomes may help suitable buyers imagine success without assuming an outlier is normal.

**Check:** Do not invent a median, treat selected testimonials as a sample, or hide failures from the defined population.

**Measure:** Expectation accuracy, qualified purchase, first-value success, refunds, and outcome-related support.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis. More realistic outcomes may reduce some purchases while improving suitability and retained economics.

**Sources:** Application hypothesis; no direct source assigned.

**Related tactics:** CPY-016, PRF-007, PRF-009

## PAT-032 — Denominator-first proof

**Family:** evidence

**Use when:** Large counts or percentages look impressive but leave buyers uncertain about the relevant population.

**Apply:** Put the denominator, time window, eligible group, and definition beside quantitative proof. For continued-use claims, show mature cohorts and distinguish registered accounts, active users, and paid customers.

**Example:** Illustrative structure: “[Verified number] of [eligible number] teams completed [defined task] within [window].”

**Mechanism:** Clear reference populations may make evidence interpretable and prevent an inflated impression from an isolated number.

**Check:** Check cohort maturity, exclusions, duplicate counting, and permission to publish aggregates. Do not convert a count into a rate without a valid denominator.

**Measure:** Proof comprehension, qualified purchase, trust questions, and later expectation mismatch.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis. This is a precision and credibility intervention, not a claim that adding denominators always increases conversion.

**Sources:** Application hypothesis; no direct source assigned.

**Related tactics:** PRF-015, PRF-019, PRF-003

## PAT-033 — Situation-matched identification

**Family:** evidence

**Use when:** Available customer stories concern organizations or tasks that the current buyer finds hard to identify with.

**Apply:** Let buyers choose a relevant task, role, operating stage, or team size, then show an approved case matching that situation. State material differences rather than implying identical outcomes.

**Example:** Illustrative selector: “Evaluating for a solo practice or a shared team?” routes to the corresponding verified workflow story.

**Mechanism:** Recognizing a similar situation may make evidence easier to apply to one’s own decision.

**Check:** Use declared business needs, not inferred sensitive traits. Avoid claiming that another customer’s result predicts this buyer’s outcome.

**Measure:** Case relevance, qualified evaluation, paid conversion, and implementation success for the declared segment.

**Evidence:** Application hypothesis informed by reference-group context in PE-021; hotel conservation research does not validate SaaS case matching.

**Sources:** PE-021

**Related tactics:** PRF-001, POS-012, INT-020

## PAT-034 — Freshness and version context

**Family:** evidence

**Use when:** Buyers cannot tell whether a review, benchmark, or screenshot still describes the current product.

**Check:** Do not reset trial eligibility, deadlines, progress, or charges deceptively. Recontacting former users requires explicit authorization and an appropriate basis.

**Measure:** Return-to-value and paid reactivation, repeated failure, unwanted-contact complaints, and retention.

**Evidence:** Research-linked mechanism: PE-024 studies temporal landmarks and aspirational behavior; software reactivation remains an application hypothesis.
