# 360 practical SaaS conversion tactics

These are concrete implementation hypotheses, selected by an observed buyer obstacle. They are additional to the skill's existing annual-first and annual monthly-equivalent baseline checks. They are not 360 guaranteed effects or instructions to apply every tactic.

Each entry identifies a trigger, exact change, proposed mechanism, check, measure, and action scope. A commercial change alters an offer or its economics; an outreach action involves contacting people. Use the user's actual authorization and verified product facts.

See [the selection workflow](references/tactic-library.md), [behavioral evidence](references/behavioral-evidence.md), and [the main skill](SKILL.md).

## Intent

### INT-001 — Match the promise at entry

**Use when:** A paid campaign promises one outcome but lands on a generic product introduction.

**Change:** Give each approved campaign a landing variant that repeats its specific outcome, audience and eligibility in the first view. Carry any material offer condition into the page rather than making visitors rediscover it.

**Why it may help:** Recognition may reduce the effort of confirming that the promised offer is here.

**Check:** Use only verified campaign claims; preserve a useful default for missing or invalid campaign parameters.

**Measure:** Paid subscriptions per campaign entrant; misleading-offer complaints.

**Action scope:** presentation

### INT-002 — Route searchers by decision stage

**Use when:** Educational, comparison and purchase queries all lead to the same sales-heavy page.

**Change:** Create distinct entry routes for learning the problem, comparing solutions and choosing an offer. Give each route the information needed for that stage and a clear bridge to the next, without forcing account creation.

**Why it may help:** Matching readiness may reduce resistance from asking for commitment too early.

**Check:** Keep essential pricing accessible from every route; do not infer readiness from sensitive characteristics.

**Measure:** Qualified progression and paid conversion by entry intent; early exits.

**Action scope:** journey

### INT-003 — Deep-link to the promised capability

**Use when:** An integration or feature advertisement opens the homepage and makes visitors search again.

**Change:** Send the verified feature-specific link to the relevant section or page, with the capability explanation visible on arrival. Retain product identity and a route to the full offer so the visitor can evaluate the larger product.

**Why it may help:** Continuity may prevent the attention loss caused by repeating a search.

**Check:** Test the destination on direct load, mobile and back navigation; avoid anchors hidden under fixed headers.

**Measure:** Paid conversion from feature entries; immediate return-to-source rate.

**Action scope:** journey

### INT-004 — Build factual competitor evaluation pages

**Use when:** Qualified visitors search for a named competitor alternative but find only broad marketing claims.

**Change:** Create a dated comparison using real buyer criteria, verified differences and the situations each product suits. Include migration prerequisites and links to the underlying facts, so the page answers an actual replacement decision.

**Why it may help:** A relevant comparison can give buyers a usable reference for evaluating differentiation.

**Check:** Recheck competitor facts before publication; include meaningful weaknesses and avoid fabricated disadvantages or fake discounts.

**Measure:** Qualified purchases from comparison traffic; mismatch refunds and corrections.

**Action scope:** presentation

### INT-005 — Bridge instructional content to product use

**Use when:** Tutorial readers receive useful advice but never see how the product performs that same task.

**Change:** At the point where a tutorial's manual process becomes repetitive, show the equivalent supported product workflow and its input/output. Let readers continue the free instructions while offering a relevant route into the product.

**Why it may help:** A contextual bridge may turn existing task motivation into product consideration.

**Check:** Keep the tutorial useful on its own; do not introduce an unrelated lead magnet or exaggerate manual difficulty.

**Measure:** Activated paid accounts from tutorial readers; content task completion.

**Action scope:** presentation

### INT-006 — Target a genuine buying event

**Use when:** The page addresses an abstract audience rather than the event that makes them seek software now.

**Change:** Build an entry page around a documented trigger such as a first client handoff, a growing approval queue or an upcoming reporting cycle. Explain what changes during that event and how the current product helps.

**Why it may help:** Situation relevance may make the benefit feel immediate without manufactured urgency.

**Check:** Use real recurring buyer situations; do not invent a personal deadline or imply knowledge of an individual's circumstances.

**Measure:** Qualified paid conversion from trigger pages; expectation mismatch.

**Action scope:** presentation

### INT-007 — Give integration visitors a direct route

**Use when:** People arriving from an integration listing must navigate several pages to confirm whether their connection is supported.

**Change:** Create an integration-specific entry that names the supported account type, data direction, setup requirements and a real example. Link directly to the appropriate evaluation path while retaining the complete product and pricing context.

**Why it may help:** Reduced compatibility uncertainty may help visitors who already know their required ecosystem.

**Check:** Verify current integration scope and plan restrictions; distinguish native integration, export and third-party automation.

**Measure:** Integration-entry paid conversion; setup failures and support burden.

**Action scope:** presentation

### INT-008 — Preserve a deliberate comparison context

**Use when:** A buyer selects comparison criteria or a use case, then loses that context when opening detail pages.

**Change:** Carry the visitor's explicit evaluation context in a shareable route or non-sensitive state. Returning to the comparison should restore the chosen criteria and location, making it easy to continue evaluating rather than rebuild the view.

**Why it may help:** Reduced memory work may support considered purchases across multiple page visits.

**Check:** Do not silently select a paid plan or store sensitive evaluation inputs; provide a way to reset the view.

**Measure:** Return-visit purchase completion; unintended-selection reports.

**Action scope:** journey

### INT-009 — Explain the referring relationship immediately

**Use when:** A partner, affiliate or invited visitor lands without understanding why the product is relevant to the referring service.

**Change:** Use an approved referral entry to explain the specific relationship and connected workflow, then present the ordinary product terms. Make any referral incentive and eligibility explicit where it affects the visitor's offer.

**Why it may help:** A coherent handoff may transfer relevant context while reducing uncertainty about the destination.

**Check:** Verify the relationship and permissions; do not imply endorsement beyond the actual agreement.

**Measure:** Referral-entry paid conversion; incentive confusion and refunds.

**Action scope:** presentation

### INT-010 — Keep marketplace and site promises aligned

**Use when:** Marketplace descriptions, screenshots and feature availability disagree with the website after product changes.

**Change:** Audit the approved marketplace listing against the current offer and update outdated capabilities, prices or setup requirements through the authorized publishing workflow. Make the landing page continue the same workflow demonstrated in the listing.

**Why it may help:** Consistent expectations may reduce abandonment caused by discovering a different offer.

**Check:** Confirm the listing's publication scope and review process; do not claim that an unapproved update is live.

**Measure:** Marketplace-referred paid activation; discrepancy complaints.

**Action scope:** presentation

### INT-011 — Make regional eligibility clear at entry

**Use when:** Visitors from unsupported regions spend time evaluating before learning that purchase or service is unavailable.

**Change:** Show supported regions, languages and relevant availability conditions near the entry information, using an editable region selector when useful. Give unsupported visitors an accurate alternative or notification option rather than a purchase path that fails later.

**Why it may help:** Early fit information may preserve trust and concentrate effort on viable buyers.

**Check:** Do not infer citizenship or use sensitive traits; verify actual availability and obtain authorization before collecting notification details.

**Measure:** Eligible paid conversion; late availability failures.

**Action scope:** presentation

### INT-012 — Continue the webinar's demonstrated workflow

**Use when:** People leaving an educational webinar arrive on a page unrelated to the workflow they just watched.

**Change:** Prepare a dated companion page containing the demonstrated workflow, current product example and relevant plan information. Preserve the promised learning material, and make the transition from example to real use explicit.

**Why it may help:** Familiarity and task continuity may help existing interest become a purchase decision.

**Check:** Use only licensed material and actual webinar claims; do not invent attendee proof or send follow-ups without authorization.

**Measure:** Webinar-entry activation and paid conversion; promise-related support.

**Action scope:** presentation

### INT-013 — Recover expired campaign destinations honestly

**Use when:** Old campaign links lead to an expired offer, an error or a misleading still-active promotion.

**Change:** Keep the destination useful by stating that the dated offer ended and presenting the current verified offer. Preserve the original topic and explain material differences so an interested visitor can still make a decision.

**Why it may help:** A clear recovery path may salvage qualified demand without bait-and-switch confusion.

**Check:** Do not silently retain an expired discount or restart its timer; check all linked terms and checkout identifiers.

**Measure:** Paid purchases from expired links; promotion disputes.

**Action scope:** journey

### INT-014 — Carry desktop evaluation across devices

**Use when:** A desktop-only product receives mobile visitors who cannot install or use it immediately.

**Change:** Offer a user-controlled way to continue on a suitable device, such as copying a stable setup link or displaying a QR code where appropriate. Preserve the chosen workflow without implying that the mobile device can run the product.

**Why it may help:** Reducing the cost of postponement may keep genuine intent from disappearing between devices.

**Check:** Do not auto-email or create accounts; exclude private data and unsupported device claims from shared links.

**Measure:** Cross-device activated purchases; broken handoffs.

**Action scope:** journey

### INT-015 — Qualify the input the product needs

**Use when:** Entry traffic includes people who lack an essential data source, permission or supported file type.

**Change:** State the required input beside the use case before the purchase path, with one recognizable example. Provide a preparation route when the missing prerequisite can be obtained, and a clear limitation when it cannot.

**Why it may help:** Early qualification may reduce wasted evaluation and subsequent purchase regret.

**Check:** Verify that the prerequisite is truly required now; avoid making optional setup sound mandatory.

**Measure:** Qualified paid activation; prerequisite-related refunds.

**Action scope:** presentation

### INT-016 — Keep shared links understandable without history

**Use when:** A prospect opens a colleague's product link but sees a page that assumes prior navigation or private context.

**Change:** Make shared evaluation URLs open a self-contained view with product identity, the referenced example and its assumptions. Include enough context to understand the comparison without exposing the original visitor's private inputs or account state.

**Why it may help:** Independent comprehension may help interest spread across a buying group.

**Check:** Validate public permissions and URL contents; require authentication for genuinely private artifacts.

**Measure:** Shared-link qualified purchases; accidental exposure or confusion.

**Action scope:** journey

### INT-017 — Explain legitimate product renames at entry

**Use when:** Qualified visitors arrive through an older product name and cannot tell whether the unfamiliar destination is still the service they intended.

**Change:** Maintain an accurate old-name entry explanation and route legacy links to the matching current product or capability. State the rename or ownership change plainly, preserving useful evaluation context instead of sending every old link to an unexplained homepage.

**Why it may help:** Continuity of identity may reduce uncertainty and distrust during an otherwise legitimate buying journey.

**Check:** Verify the actual relationship and current availability; do not imply unchanged ownership, contracts or features when those also changed.

**Measure:** Paid conversion from legacy-name entries; identity and destination confusion.

**Action scope:** journey

### INT-018 — Present a useful destination after public sharing

**Use when:** Public reports, templates or exported artifacts attract viewers, but their product link lands on an unexplained signup screen.

**Change:** Create a viewer landing that explains how the shared artifact was made and offers a matching sample workflow. Let visitors inspect the original public artifact before deciding whether to create their own.

**Why it may help:** Seeing a relevant outcome may make the product's value concrete for new entrants.

**Check:** Respect the creator's sharing permissions and branding settings; never expose private content as acquisition material.

**Measure:** Viewer-to-paid activation; sharing-related privacy complaints.

**Action scope:** journey

### INT-019 — Respect an explicit language selection

**Use when:** Visitors choose a language but campaign redirects or page navigation revert them to an unfamiliar version.

**Change:** Preserve the declared language across marketing, pricing and entry routes, with a visible way to change it. Where the product itself has narrower language support, state that boundary before purchase rather than implying complete localization.

**Why it may help:** Reduced comprehension effort may help visitors finish a considered evaluation.

**Check:** Verify translated prices and terms against the source; do not imply unsupported product languages or use unreliable auto-translation for commitments.

**Measure:** Paid conversion by supported language; translation-related support.

**Action scope:** journey

### INT-020 — Match specialist traffic to specialist evidence

**Use when:** A technical article attracts practitioners, but its destination removes the API, architecture or workflow details they came to evaluate.

**Change:** Provide a specialist entry with the relevant working example, current documentation and deployment prerequisites, alongside a short explanation for nontechnical collaborators. Retain the normal offer path without requiring a sales conversation to inspect basic fit.

**Why it may help:** Depth appropriate to intent may turn technical credibility into qualified buying interest.

**Check:** Keep examples compatible with the actual product version; avoid exposing secrets or implying production readiness from a toy example.

**Measure:** Technically qualified paid activation; integration abandonment.

**Action scope:** presentation


## Positioning

### POS-001 — Lead with a concrete customer outcome

**Use when:** The first headline names a broad technology or ambition without explaining what the buyer gets done.

**Change:** Rewrite the headline around one supported job and result, such as turning scattered requests into an assigned work queue. Use the adjacent sentence to identify the intended customer and keep broad brand language in a supporting role.

**Why it may help:** A recognizable outcome may help qualified visitors identify relevance quickly.

**Check:** Confirm the product actually delivers the named result; avoid invented time or money savings.

**Measure:** Qualified paid conversion; wrong-fit registrations.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-002 — Explain the mechanism behind the promise

**Use when:** A strong benefit claim appears without any explanation of how the product produces it.

**Change:** Follow the main outcome with a short mechanism statement naming the actual input, processing step and useful output. For example, explain that the product groups incoming messages and assigns owners rather than saying only that it makes teams efficient.

**Why it may help:** A plausible causal explanation may make a benefit easier to believe and remember.

**Check:** Use current capabilities and honest dependencies; do not imply autonomous action where user review is required.

**Measure:** Benefit comprehension and paid activation; expectation mismatch.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-003 — Choose a defensible product differentiator

**Use when:** The page lists many ordinary features and gives buyers no reason to prefer this product.

**Change:** Identify a verified capability or operating advantage that matters to the priority buyer and differs from their real alternatives. Give that distinction a prominent explanation and a concrete demonstration, while keeping shared category basics concise.

**Why it may help:** A meaningful contrast may reduce the feeling that all options are interchangeable.

**Check:** Validate the differentiator against current alternatives; avoid unsupported superlatives or exclusive claims.

**Measure:** Qualified purchases; competitor-comparison objections.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-004 — Say who should choose another option

**Use when:** Buyers with unsuitable needs repeatedly register, request refunds or require capabilities the product lacks.

**Change:** Add a concise fit boundary explaining cases where the product is unsuitable and, when appropriate, what category of solution those customers need. Place it near the relevant use case so suitable buyers can recognize their own fit more confidently.

**Why it may help:** Honest disqualification may strengthen credibility and improve customer selection.

**Check:** Base exclusions on actual product limits or needs, never demographic stereotypes; do not turn the boundary into dismissive copy.

**Measure:** Retained paid conversion; wrong-fit refunds and support.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-005 — Name the deliverable before the feature list

**Use when:** Visitors understand the broad benefit but cannot picture what exists after they use the product.

**Change:** Show the actual deliverable type in the core proposition: an exportable report, an approved schedule, a reconciled list or a monitored workflow. Include one representative output and say what the buyer can do with it next.

**Why it may help:** A concrete end state may make an otherwise abstract software purchase easier to evaluate.

**Check:** Use real output formats and permissions; label examples and distinguish generated drafts from verified final work.

**Measure:** Paid activation into the named output; output-quality complaints.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-006 — Compare with the real current workaround

**Use when:** The page positions itself against competitors while most buyers currently use spreadsheets, email or manual work.

**Change:** Explain the specific handoffs or repeated steps the product changes relative to the current workaround. Preserve an honest account of what the old method does well, so the comparison addresses the buyer's actual decision to adopt software.

**Why it may help:** A familiar reference may make the incremental benefit more understandable.

**Check:** Use observed workflows; do not invent a labor cost or suggest every manual process must be replaced.

**Measure:** First-time software purchases; adoption abandonment.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-007 — Draw the product's workflow boundary

**Use when:** Buyers cannot tell where the tool starts, what it handles or what they must still do elsewhere.

**Change:** Add a simple input-to-output workflow showing the product's own steps and the adjacent tools or human work. Identify required handoffs and the final destination rather than implying that the product solves the entire business process.

**Why it may help:** A clear boundary may reduce uncertainty about fit and implementation effort.

**Check:** Keep integrations and responsibilities accurate; do not represent planned connections as working ones.

**Measure:** Qualified activation and paid conversion; boundary-related support.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-008 — Make human control a visible benefit

**Use when:** An AI or automation product is described in ways that make prospects fear uncontrolled actions.

**Change:** Explain the existing review, approval and permission boundaries in the main product story. Show which actions are suggestions, which require confirmation and which can run automatically under an explicit configuration, using one realistic workflow example.

**Why it may help:** Perceived control may make useful automation easier to evaluate and adopt.

**Check:** Verify the actual permissions and failure behavior; never promise human approval if the product bypasses it.

**Measure:** Paid activation; unwanted-action incidents and complaints.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-009 — Separate purchaser benefits from user benefits

**Use when:** The person paying is different from the daily user, but the page addresses only one of them.

**Change:** Present a short benefit pair for the same workflow: what becomes easier for the user and what becomes more manageable for the purchaser. Connect both to actual capabilities without making the end user sound like a problem to control.

**Why it may help:** Recognizing both perspectives may help a buying relationship reach a shared decision.

**Check:** Do not invent monitoring, reporting or family/team permissions; preserve the user's autonomy and actual account terms.

**Measure:** Purchases involving a separate payer; setup disputes.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-010 — Give one workflow the main story

**Use when:** An all-in-one product presents several unrelated use cases with equal weight, leaving the page hard to summarize.

**Change:** Choose the most relevant supported workflow for the current entry page and organize the main explanation around it. Move secondary use cases into a clearly labeled exploration section so buyers can still discover breadth after understanding the core value.

**Why it may help:** A focused mental model may make a broad product easier to remember and assess.

**Check:** Use evidence about the page's intended audience; do not hide a limitation or imply the selected workflow is universal.

**Measure:** Qualified paid conversion; use-case mismatch.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-011 — Position as a complement when accurate

**Use when:** Prospects assume adopting the product requires replacing a tool they already trust or have invested in.

**Change:** State clearly when the product works alongside the incumbent and show the precise division of work. Explain what stays in the existing tool and what the new product contributes, with a current integration or export example where available.

**Why it may help:** Lower perceived replacement cost may reduce resistance to a useful addition.

**Check:** Verify interoperability and required plans; do not say a connection is seamless when manual work is required.

**Measure:** Complementary-use purchases; integration failures and churn.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-012 — Describe fit by operating stage

**Use when:** The offer uses vague company-size labels that do not explain when a customer actually needs it.

**Change:** Describe the operational situation the product serves, such as managing work across several approvers or replacing a repeatedly maintained spreadsheet. Show the capability that becomes valuable at that stage without requiring a buyer to match a prestige label.

**Why it may help:** Recognizable operating conditions may support self-qualification better than generic status categories.

**Check:** Use actual feature limits and customer research; avoid invented minimum scale or exclusionary assumptions.

**Measure:** Qualified paid adoption; overbuying and underuse.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-013 — Separate shipped capability from roadmap

**Use when:** Future features and current features appear in one undifferentiated product story.

**Change:** Mark future capabilities as planned or in development with the appropriate uncertainty, and make the present purchase case stand on delivered features. Give buyers a concise way to verify availability before they choose an offer.

**Why it may help:** Clear temporal boundaries may reduce ambiguity and purchase regret.

**Check:** Use approved roadmap wording; do not imply dates or contractual commitments that the business has not accepted.

**Measure:** Paid conversion on current value; roadmap-related refunds.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-014 — Give recurring payment a recurring reason

**Use when:** A subscription page emphasizes a one-time setup result and leaves ongoing value unexplained.

**Change:** Show the supported work that repeats after setup: monitoring, updating, recurring preparation, collaboration or maintained access. Explain what the customer receives during ordinary continued use, rather than relying on a long feature list to justify renewal.

**Why it may help:** A visible ongoing job may help buyers understand why the product is a subscription.

**Check:** Do not manufacture recurring work or imply maintenance/services the product does not supply.

**Measure:** Subscription purchases and subsequent active use; early cancellation.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-015 — State the first payoff and prerequisites

**Use when:** Prospects see a large long-term benefit but cannot tell what a first useful session would involve.

**Change:** Describe one achievable initial result and the inputs needed to reach it. Use an evidence-based time estimate only when measured; otherwise list the actual steps and identify setup dependencies that might delay the result.

**Why it may help:** A concrete near-term payoff may make the effort of starting feel more manageable.

**Check:** Differentiate a sample success from a typical customer result; keep required access and data preparation visible.

**Measure:** First useful result and paid conversion; setup disappointment.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-016 — Make the central tradeoff explicit

**Use when:** The product makes a deliberate design choice, but marketing presents it as universally superior.

**Change:** Explain the benefit and cost of the choice, such as more customization with more setup or simpler operation with fewer advanced controls. Help the intended buyer recognize why that tradeoff may suit their priorities.

**Why it may help:** A credible two-sided explanation may reduce skepticism and attract better-fitting customers.

**Check:** Use a real product tradeoff, not a contrived weakness; do not conceal a material limitation elsewhere.

**Measure:** Retained purchases; tradeoff-related refunds and support.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-017 — Show the responsibility that changes

**Use when:** Feature descriptions explain functionality but leave the buyer unsure who will do less work or gain control.

**Change:** Map a supported capability to a concrete responsibility change, such as owners receiving assigned requests instead of a coordinator forwarding every message. Keep the responsible person visible before and after the change so the value is operational.

**Why it may help:** A responsibility-level consequence may make an abstract feature personally relevant.

**Check:** Verify the workflow and retain human review where required; avoid claiming jobs or labor costs disappear automatically.

**Measure:** Paid adoption of the workflow; missed handoffs and support.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-018 — Lead comparisons with a relevant criterion

**Use when:** Comparison pages organize every feature alphabetically, burying the criterion that matters most to the intended buyer.

**Change:** Order the comparison around a documented buying criterion the product serves well, such as review control, deployment constraints or collaboration depth. Explain why that criterion matters and retain access to other important tradeoffs.

**Why it may help:** Making a meaningful evaluation axis salient may clarify a justified preference.

**Check:** Choose the criterion from buyer needs rather than inventing an artificial category where the product alone wins.

**Measure:** Qualified purchases from comparisons; post-purchase fit.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-019 — Explain what changes as usage grows

**Use when:** The page describes an individual task but buyers worry about handling a larger real workload.

**Change:** Demonstrate the existing organizational capability that becomes useful with more work, such as bulk review, shared ownership or searchable history. Show a representative larger dataset and explain any boundaries instead of asserting unlimited scalability.

**Why it may help:** A credible future-use picture may make adoption feel useful beyond the first task.

**Check:** Verify tested operating limits; do not imply a measured throughput or scale that lacks evidence.

**Measure:** Growth-oriented paid purchases; capacity-related failures.

**Action scope:** presentation

### POS-020 — Distinguish software from managed service

**Use when:** Prospects confuse the tool with a done-for-you service and expect the vendor to perform the work.

**Change:** State who prepares inputs, makes decisions, performs the task and supports problems. If an actual service component exists, separate its scope from the software so the buyer understands the working relationship before choosing a plan.

**Why it may help:** Clear responsibility may reduce uncertainty for suitable buyers and avoid service-expectation mismatch.

**Check:** Use current service terms and actual staffing; introducing managed work is a commercial proposal, not a copy edit.

**Measure:** Qualified paid conversion; service-expectation refunds.

**Action scope:** presentation


## Copy

### CPY-001 — Use the customer's task vocabulary

**Use when:** The site describes familiar work with internal product jargon that prospects do not use.

**Change:** Replace internal labels in the main explanation with terms found in actual customer questions, search queries or support conversations. Keep necessary product names, but connect them to the recognizable task the first time they appear.

**Why it may help:** Familiar language may reduce translation effort and make the offer easier to recognize.

**Check:** Use research as vocabulary evidence, not fabricated customer quotations; confirm that simpler wording preserves meaning.

**Measure:** Offer comprehension and qualified paid conversion; terminology confusion.

**Action scope:** presentation

### CPY-002 — Replace praise with observable verbs

**Use when:** Descriptions rely on words such as powerful, effortless or revolutionary without explaining behavior.

**Change:** Rewrite each central claim as something the customer can observe the product doing, such as grouping duplicate requests or exporting an approved report. Retain a short explanation of why that action matters to the buyer's work.

**Why it may help:** Specific actions may be easier to evaluate than promotional adjectives.

**Check:** Demonstrate the named behavior in the current product; avoid turning a potential benefit into a guaranteed result.

**Measure:** Paid activation into the stated capability; expectation mismatch.

**Action scope:** presentation

### CPY-003 — Attach the condition to the benefit

**Use when:** A headline benefit is true only for some workloads, but its necessary condition appears far away.

**Change:** Put the material condition next to the benefit in plain language, such as explaining that a workflow needs a supported data source. Preserve an appealing main statement while making it clear when that statement applies.

**Why it may help:** Conditional specificity may make a claim more credible and help buyers assess fit.

**Check:** Identify conditions from actual product behavior; do not use tiny qualifiers to rescue an otherwise misleading headline.

**Measure:** Qualified purchases; condition-related refunds.

**Action scope:** presentation

### CPY-004 — Use a concrete before-and-after sentence

**Use when:** A feature list leaves visitors to infer how their everyday work would change.

**Change:** Write one concise before-and-after comparison using a real task: describe the current repeated step, then the supported new workflow. Keep the comparison on the same task and outcome rather than switching to a more favorable example.

**Why it may help:** A contrast in recognizable work may make the benefit easier to mentally simulate.

**Check:** Verify both workflows and include required review or setup; do not invent customer time savings.

**Measure:** Benefit comprehension and paid conversion; workflow mismatch.

**Action scope:** presentation

### CPY-005 — Frame an evidenced cost of delay

**Use when:** The product solves a costly recurring problem, but the page describes only optional convenience.

**Change:** Explain the documented consequence of leaving that problem unresolved, such as requests remaining unassigned or reports becoming outdated. Pair the consequence with the actual supported remedy so the buyer can judge whether action matters now.

**Why it may help:** A concrete loss frame may make an existing problem more salient.

**Check:** Use verified consequences or explicitly labeled assumptions; avoid fear, invented financial losses and unsupported personal claims.

**Measure:** Qualified purchases; anxiety or misleading-claim complaints.

**Action scope:** presentation

### CPY-006 — Offer an attainable gain frame

**Use when:** The page repeatedly emphasizes mistakes and failure, while buyers struggle to picture a useful positive outcome.

**Change:** Rewrite a central explanation around the achievable benefit of the supported workflow, such as knowing which request needs attention. Keep material risk information available, but let the main story show what better operation looks like.

**Why it may help:** An attainable gain may make starting feel constructive rather than threatening.

**Check:** Do not imply guaranteed success or conceal the effort needed; compare outcome quality, not merely copy preference.

**Measure:** Paid activation; disappointment and avoidance-related exits.

**Action scope:** presentation

### CPY-007 — Let buyers picture the first ordinary day

**Use when:** The marketing remains abstract even after visitors understand the feature names.

**Change:** Describe a short, realistic sequence of using the product during an ordinary workday: what arrives, what the person sees, what they decide and what happens next. Use actual interface behavior and preserve any manual work.

**Why it may help:** Mental simulation may help a buyer connect features to personal use.

**Check:** Label the sequence as an example; avoid unsupported timing, automation or lifestyle promises.

**Measure:** Use-case comprehension and paid activation; expectation mismatch.

**Action scope:** presentation

### CPY-008 — Translate an abstract unit with arithmetic

**Use when:** The offer uses requests, credits or operations that buyers cannot map to a recognizable task.

**Change:** Add a worked example with explicit assumptions, such as ten items using three operations each consuming thirty operations. Keep the actual unit as the entitlement and explain which additional actions can consume more, so the example remains conditional.

**Why it may help:** Concrete quantities may make an unfamiliar value metric easier to evaluate.

**Check:** Use the real counting rule; do not present example task counts as guaranteed completed outcomes.

**Measure:** Plan-fit purchases; allowance confusion and early exhaustion.

**Action scope:** presentation

### CPY-009 — Use a bounded explanatory analogy

**Use when:** A novel product category is difficult to explain because visitors lack a familiar frame of reference.

**Change:** Introduce one accurate analogy, such as an inbox for incoming requests, then name the important differences and real behavior. Use the analogy to orient the buyer before presenting precise features, rather than asking them to understand a newly invented category name.

**Why it may help:** A familiar conceptual structure may reduce the effort of understanding an unfamiliar product.

**Check:** Choose an analogy that preserves the relevant relationships; explicitly correct likely false inferences and avoid suggesting nonexistent capabilities.

**Measure:** Product comprehension and qualified paid conversion; analogy-driven misconceptions.

**Action scope:** presentation

### CPY-010 — Keep qualifications beside numeric claims

**Use when:** A strong number appears in the main copy while its population, period or scope is hidden elsewhere.
