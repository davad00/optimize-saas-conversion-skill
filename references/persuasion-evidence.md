# Persuasion evidence register

This register supports the 66 entries in [persuasion-catalog.jsonl](persuasion-catalog.jsonl). The original 22 entries are broad mechanisms; the additional 44 are distinct applied decision patterns. They are not 66 independently validated laws or 66 measured SaaS conversion effects. Research checked on 14 November 2026.

## How to use evidence

Read the `evidence` field before citing a pattern. A nonempty `source_ids` list links a mechanism or requirement to a source; it does not mean the exact implementation was tested there. Empty `source_ids` means an original application hypothesis with no specific external study claimed. Cite project observations separately and mark any measured site effect with its population, outcome, window, and uncertainty.

Source summaries below are deliberately narrow. Bibliographic identity and available abstracts or full text were checked; some publisher full texts are access-restricted. Do not imply that every full paper was reviewed in depth. Reopen a source when a stronger or more specific claim is needed. No study effect size is imported as a predicted sales uplift.

## Source register

### PE-001 — Anchoring and reference judgments

**Source:** [Tversky & Kahneman (1974), Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124).

**Context:** Original research article on judgment heuristics, including anchoring.

**Use and limit:** A relevant reference can motivate a price-comparison hypothesis. The paper does not identify an optimal SaaS price or justify an inflated anchor.

### PE-002 — Gain and loss framing

**Source:** [Kahneman & Tversky (1979), Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk](https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185).

**Context:** Original theoretical and experimental treatment of decisions under risk and reference-dependent valuation.

**Use and limit:** Describe real trade-offs. Do not claim a universal loss-to-gain multiplier or transfer an effect size to subscription purchases.

### PE-003 — Choice context

**Source:** [Huber, Payne & Puto (1982), Adding Asymmetrically Dominated Alternatives: Violations of Regularity and the Similarity Hypothesis](https://doi.org/10.1086/208899).

**Context:** Original experiments examining how an added alternative can change choice probabilities.

**Use and limit:** Use real useful packages. A change in plan share does not show incremental demand, contribution, or retention.

### PE-004 — Defaults

**Source:** [Johnson & Goldstein (2003), Do Defaults Save Lives?](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14631022/).

**Context:** Original research on default settings in organ-donation decisions.

**Use and limit:** A visible reversible SaaS default is an application hypothesis. Preserve explicit preferences and never treat a default as authorization to charge.

### PE-005 — Mental accounting

**Source:** [Thaler (1985), Mental Accounting and Consumer Choice](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.4.3.199).

**Context:** Original model connecting the framing and accounting of gains and losses with consumer decisions.

**Use and limit:** Use a comprehensible budget unit while retaining the real total, schedule, and commitment. The model does not validate a particular price presentation.

### PE-006 — Progress toward goals

**Source:** [Nunes & Drèze (2006), The Endowed Progress Effect: How Artificial Advancement Increases Effort](https://doi.org/10.1086/500480).

**Context:** Original research on progress and persistence toward a reward.

**Use and limit:** Credit only genuine completed or automatically performed work in product flows. Do not manufacture fake progress or unnecessary tasks.

### PE-007 — Cue-linked action planning

**Source:** [Gollwitzer (1999), Implementation Intentions: Strong Effects of Simple Plans](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.54.7.493).

**Context:** Author synthesis and theoretical account of research on linking situations to intended actions.

**Use and limit:** A voluntary first-task or obstacle-response plan is a SaaS application. It does not authorize scheduling, reminders, or customer outreach.

### PE-008 — Participation and valuation

**Source:** [Norton, Mochon & Ariely (2012), The IKEA Effect: When Labor Leads to Love](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/norton%20mochon%20ariely_6f7b1134-06ef-4940-a2a5-ba1b3be7e47e.pdf).

**Context:** Original experiments on valuation of self-created objects and boundaries including successful completion.

**Use and limit:** Meaningful creation may demonstrate value. Extra work, failed setup, and holding artifacts hostage are not established beneficial applications.

### PE-009 — Sequential requests

**Source:** [Freedman & Fraser (1966), Compliance without Pressure: The Foot-in-the-Door Technique](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5969145/).

**Context:** Original experiments involving small requests followed by larger requests.

**Use and limit:** Use a relevant voluntary task as an adoption step. A small action is not consent to an undisclosed commercial commitment.

### PE-010 — Social influence

**Source:** [Muchnik, Aral & Taylor (2013), Social Influence Bias: A Randomized Experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23929980/).

**Context:** Original randomized experiment involving online ratings.

**Use and limit:** Authentic proof can motivate a hypothesis. Ratings behavior does not supply a testimonial, logo-strip, or SaaS purchase uplift.

### PE-011 — Choice complexity

**Source:** [Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010), Can There Ever Be Too Many Options? A Meta-Analytic Review of Choice Overload](https://doi.org/10.1086/651235).

**Context:** Original meta-analysis reporting variation across choice-overload studies. See also Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman (2015), Choice Overload: A Conceptual Review and Meta-analysis, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002, for moderators.

**Use and limit:** Diagnose task complexity, uncertainty, and relevant criteria. Neither source prescribes exactly three plans or proves that fewer options improve this site.

### PE-012 — Scarcity and value

**Source:** [Lynn (1991), Scarcity Effects on Value: A Quantitative Review of the Commodity Theory Literature](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/entities/publication/167c0da3-fa55-44ad-be11-f258914ee9a3).

**Context:** Original quantitative review of scarcity and perceived value/desirability.

**Use and limit:** Explain an actual capacity limit. The source does not establish an evergreen countdown, fake stock count, or SaaS conversion percentage.

### PE-013 — Process simulation

**Source:** [Pham & Taylor (1999), From Thought to Action: Effects of Process-Versus Outcome-Based Mental Simulations on Performance](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167299025002010).

**Context:** Original study contrasting process and outcome simulations in college students preparing for an examination; it reported benefits of process simulation in that setting.

**Use and limit:** A product rehearsal or internal buying explanation is a new application. The study did not test SaaS onboarding, sales briefs, or checkout conversion.

### PE-014 — Narrative mental simulation

**Source:** [Escalas (2004), Imagine Yourself in the Product: Mental Simulation, Narrative Transportation, and Persuasion](https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.2004.10639163).

**Context:** Original advertising research developing and examining mental simulation through narrative transportation.

**Use and limit:** Use realistic, labeled product stories to aid evaluation. Narrative engagement is not equivalent to truthful evidence, product fit, or paid conversion.

### PE-015 — Limits of imagery appeals

**Source:** [Petrova & Cialdini (2005), Fluency of Consumption Imagery and the Backfire Effects of Imagery Appeals](https://doi.org/10.1086/497556).

**Context:** Original research on consumption imagery and conditions under which imagery appeals can backfire.

**Use and limit:** Make scenarios concrete and easy to evaluate. Do not assume asking buyers to imagine success reliably increases preference or purchase.

### PE-016 — Structural alignment in comparison

**Source:** [Zhang & Markman (1998), Overcoming the Early Entrant Advantage: The Role of Alignable and Nonalignable Differences](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224379803500401).

**Context:** Three original experiments on how comparable versus noncomparable brand attributes affect memory and evaluation.

**Use and limit:** Align comparable units and genuine differences. The findings do not establish a conversion effect for a particular pricing table or workflow demo.

### PE-017 — Two-sided messages

**Source:** [Golden & Alpert (1987), Comparative Analysis of the Relative Effectiveness of One- and Two-Sided Communication for Contrasting Products](https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.1987.10673056).

**Context:** Original study comparing communication strategies for mass transit and deodorant; the abstract explicitly cautions against broad cross-product generalization.

**Use and limit:** Disclose a material limitation because it matters to the decision. A two-sided message is not automatically more credible or effective for every product.

### PE-018 — Opportunity costs

**Source:** [Frederick, Novemsky, Wang, Dhar & Nowlis (2009), Opportunity Cost Neglect](https://doi.org/10.1086/599764).

**Context:** Original consumer experiments on cues to consider displaced alternatives; such cues could reduce purchases or favor more affordable options.

**Use and limit:** Use buyer-directed comparison of real alternatives. This is not an always-positive conversion tactic; monitor fit, contribution, and subsequent use.

### PE-019 — Self-efficacy

**Source:** [Bandura (1977), Self-efficacy: Toward a Unifying Theory of Behavioral Change](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/847061/).

**Context:** Original theoretical article concerning efficacy expectations and behavioral change, drawing on research in behavioral settings.

**Use and limit:** Supported mastery can motivate an onboarding hypothesis. Do not borrow therapeutic effects, claim clinical benefits, or treat tutorial completion as independent competence.

### PE-020 — Cognitive offloading

**Source:** [Risko & Gilbert (2016), Cognitive Offloading](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27542527/).

**Context:** Author review of the mechanisms and consequences of using external action or resources to reduce cognitive demands.

**Use and limit:** A saved comparison record may reduce memory demands; the review does not directly test SaaS purchasing, shared briefs, or their commercial effects.

### PE-021 — Reference-group norms

**Source:** [Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius (2008), A Room with a Viewpoint: Using Social Norms to Motivate Environmental Conservation in Hotels](https://doi.org/10.1086/586910).

**Context:** Original field experiments on hotel towel reuse and descriptive norms, including situation-matched reference groups.

**Use and limit:** Publish only genuine relevant group behavior. Hotel conservation outcomes cannot establish a SaaS adoption or purchase effect.

### PE-022 — Elimination by aspects

**Source:** [Tversky (1972), Choice by Elimination](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-2496(72)90011-9).

**Context:** Original mathematical choice-model paper characterizing choice through elimination by aspects.

**Use and limit:** A transparent must-have filter is an applied decision aid. The descriptive model is not evidence that any filter increases conversion or that excluded options are objectively inferior.

### PE-023 — Accessible attention and feedback

**Source:** [W3C, Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/).

**Context:** Official accessibility standard covering relevant contrast, color, keyboard, focus, reflow, input, and status requirements.

**Use and limit:** Apply the relevant criteria to the actual interaction. A contrast result or accessible status message is not a complete accessibility audit or a revenue forecast.

### PE-024 — Temporal landmarks

**Source:** [Dai, Milkman & Riis (2014), The Fresh Start Effect: Temporal Landmarks Motivate Aspirational Behavior](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901).

**Context:** Original research relating meaningful temporal landmarks to aspirational behaviors; an author-hosted paper is available at https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf.

**Use and limit:** A real new project or planning cycle can motivate a reentry hypothesis. Do not manufacture deadlines, reset commercial conditions, or claim lasting SaaS retention from goal initiation.

### PE-025 — Tradeoff contrast and extremeness aversion

**Source:** [Simonson & Tversky (1992), Choice in Context: Tradeoff Contrast and Extremeness Aversion](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224379202900301); [author-institution abstract](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/choice-context-tradeoff-contrast-extremeness-aversion).

**Context:** Original consumer-product choice studies examining comparisons among tradeoffs and the appeal of intermediate versus extreme options. The paper distinguishes attraction and compromise effects.

**Use and limit:** This is the relevant source for the compromise part of PAT-007; PE-003 separately concerns asymmetric dominance. Neither source prescribes three SaaS tiers, validates an artificial package, or establishes incremental demand, contribution or retention.

## Application limits

A pattern must address an observed obstacle and fit the real offer. A clear total may discourage an unsuitable purchase; a cheaper plan may fit better; a transparent limitation may disqualify a prospect. Evaluate qualified paid outcomes, delivered value, retained economics, refunds, and support burden together.

Commercial offers, price changes, experiments, publishing, and customer outreach must remain within the authorization already provided. Draft and verify concrete work before asking for missing authorization. No psychological mechanism expands the permitted action scope.

Use the [behavioral evidence guide](behavioral-evidence.md) and [source register](sources.md) for additional technical and methodological context. Preserve the mandatory pricing and baseline checks in the main skill.
