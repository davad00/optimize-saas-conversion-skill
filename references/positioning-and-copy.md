# Positioning, copy, and page structure

Use this as a drafting and diagnosis playbook. Treat messaging changes as hypotheses until buyer behavior supports them.

## Contents

- Define the buying situation
- Build a message map
- Make the hero explain the purchase
- Choose the page sequence by buyer uncertainty
- Write features as capabilities with consequences

- Offer and risk reversal
- Copy acceptance check

## Define the buying situation

Write a positioning sentence for internal use:

"For [specific customer in a specific situation], [product] helps achieve [valuable outcome] through [credible mechanism], compared with [real alternative], with [verified differentiator]."

Choose the customer's main alternative honestly: manual work, spreadsheets, outsourcing, an incumbent, another tool, or doing nothing. Do not force a competitor comparison where the decision is whether to solve the problem at all.

Identify the user, buyer, approver, and blocker. A developer may need an API example; their manager may need operating cost and access controls. Give each the evidence needed without making the entry page explain every possible feature.

Inventory proof before writing claims. Prefer real product demonstration, verifiable capability, documented case study, contextual customer quote, and permitted logo use. A logo without a described relationship is weak evidence. If proof is unavailable, explain the mechanism or demonstrate the product.

## Build a message map

For each priority audience, connect:

| Buyer concern | Claim | Proof needed | Placement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "Does this solve my actual task?" | Concrete outcome and mechanism | Real workflow or output | Hero and first demonstration |
| "Will it work with our setup?" | Verified integration and limits | Supported integration, example, docs | Near relevant capability |
| "Is it worth the cost?" | Value relative to a realistic alternative | Transparent assumptions or case evidence | Pricing and business case |
| "Will adoption be difficult?" | Clear setup and support | Actual onboarding steps and resources | Before commitment |
| "Can I trust this?" | Relevant security/support/reliability facts | Current documentation | Decision points and procurement |
| "What am I committing to?" | Clear trial, billing, renewal, cancellation | Actual policy and configured behavior | CTA and checkout |

Never convert aspirations into present-tense capabilities. Avoid "save 10 hours" unless the evidence or explicitly editable calculator assumptions justify it.

## Make the hero explain the purchase

Use a concrete outcome headline, a short explanation of who/what/how, a primary action, and a product visual or proof when useful.

Useful headline directions:

- Outcome: "Turn support conversations into an organized product backlog."
- Task: "Send client proposals with approvals and billing in one workflow."
- Alternative: "Review contract changes without merging comments by hand."
- Role: "Usage billing built for API teams."

These are fictional copy patterns, not claims about the user's product. Rewrite them using verified capabilities.

Match the CTA to the real next step:

| Intent and actual destination | Clear pattern |
| --- | --- |
| Immediate use without payment | "Create your workspace" or "Start free" when genuinely free |
| Time-limited trial | "Start your 14-day trial" when the configured term is 14 days |
| Human consultation | "Book a demo" with expectations for the meeting |
| Product preview | "Explore the demo" when an actual demo opens |
| Paid checkout | "Choose Pro" or "Continue to checkout" with a clear selected price |

Keep button text compact; place price, card requirement, and commitment information adjacent where relevant. Do not promise no card when the next step requests one. Do not optimize text in isolation from the next screen.

A primary CTA can recur after meaningful evidence. Use a secondary action for a genuinely different readiness level, such as a demo for visitors not ready to start. Avoid equally loud competing actions that obscure the intended path.

## Choose the page sequence by buyer uncertainty

A practical starting sequence is outcome → demonstration → relevant proof → benefits tied to tasks → objections → offer → next step. Adapt it to actual questions:

- For a simple low-cost tool, keep the path direct and let people try it.
- For a complex B2B product, show workflow, integrations, implementation, security, and a buyer-ready explanation.
- For a comparison page, compare real criteria and date-sensitive facts fairly; cite the source and date.
- For a paid campaign page, match the ad's audience, promise, offer, and landing destination.
- For existing users upgrading, start with the blocked valuable task and incremental benefit.

Treat long versus short pages as a context question. Provide enough decision information and navigation for the task. Do not delete useful detail merely to meet an arbitrary word count.

## Write features as capabilities with consequences

Use "You can [task], so [specific consequence]" as a drafting aid. Keep technical details where they support credibility or buying fit.

Weak: "Powerful next-generation collaboration."
Better if true: "Assign an owner to every request and see who is waiting on a reply."

Weak: "Enterprise-grade security."
Better if documented: state the supported access controls, audit features, encryption scope, or certification with appropriate links and limitations.

Use concrete nouns and verbs; cut vague superlatives. Do not invent a customer voice, fabricated quote, or false comparison to make copy sound persuasive.

## Handle objections at the point of doubt

Map objections to placement rather than dumping everything into a generic FAQ:

- Migration concern → alongside import and onboarding explanation.
- Compatibility concern → alongside supported integrations.
- Price concern → clear units, inclusions, limits, and real value rationale.
- Payment anxiety → order summary, renewal details, refund policy, and recognizable payment experience.
- Adoption concern → first useful task and actual support.
- Privacy or procurement concern → accurate documentation and a reachable contact route.

Use FAQ sections for genuine remaining questions. Keep critical billing information visible without requiring an accordion open.

## Offer and risk reversal

Make the offer more valuable before making it louder. Consider a useful template, sample output, guided setup, evaluation checklist, pilot, trial, or real refund policy when it fits the product economics.

Define a guarantee precisely: eligibility, time window, scope, process, and exclusions. Confirm it can be honored. Do not introduce a guarantee as copy without operational support.

For a savings or ROI calculator, make inputs editable, identify assumptions, use defensible arithmetic, and show costs as well as benefits. Distinguish time saved from realized cash savings. Do not frame a hypothetical result as a customer outcome.

## Copy acceptance check

Can an intended buyer answer: what is this, is it for me, what does it let me do, why should I believe it, what does it cost, and what happens next?

Check consistency across advertisement, landing page, pricing, signup, checkout, trial reminders, and product behavior. Use customer vocabulary without overfitting one quote. Verify every number, deadline, claim, and link. Evaluate actual paid or qualified outcomes with downstream guardrails, not copy preference alone.
